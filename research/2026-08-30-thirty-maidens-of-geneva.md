## Editorial verdict

The earlier angle used the girls to introduce a larger argument about French fiscal weakness. That is factually defensible and narratively wrong for this video. The title promises thirty children, so the episode must stay with them.

The revised story follows a human sequence:

1. Girls are summoned to a diplomat's reception rooms and physically counted.
2. A surviving 1782 sheet reveals thirty names and birth dates.
3. The payment rule is explained only far enough to show why being alive mattered.
4. The girls are selected, monitored, inoculated in some cases, and turned into proof-of-life paperwork.
5. They grow up, marry, travel, change names, and become harder to trace.
6. One child on the 1782 list, Pernette-Elisabeth Martin, dies at eight.
7. Another, Elisabeth-Pauline de Chapeaurouge, lives until 1862.

There is no large sovereign-risk conclusion. The ending returns to the list and lets the distance between a financial “head” and a human life do the work.

## Revised packaging

- **Thumbnail text:** THEY HAD TO STAY ALIVE
- **Thumbnail image:** The genuine 1782 list beside one child-sized silhouette. Do not use photographs of modern children or generated portraits presented as real.
- **Cold-open promise:** In October 1784, a French diplomat gathered twenty-nine girls under seven in his reception rooms because he needed to certify that they were alive.

## Central question

Why did a French diplomat repeatedly count little girls in Geneva, and what happened when the children behind those payments grew up, moved away, or died?

## Story premise

The “thirty girls of Geneva” were not one permanent cast. Genevan bankers created repeated pools, most often using thirty named lives. The surviving January 1782 list gives one cohort a concrete shape. By staying honest about the multiple lists, the video can use the famous phrase without inventing a single unchanging group.

## Viewer journey

- **Viewer begins believing:** One fixed group of thirty girls was used in a macabre bet by France.
- **Viewer leaves knowing:** Many real Genevan girls were incorporated into repeated annuity pools. They did not choose the arrangement or sign the records. Their health, location, marriages, and deaths became administrative events for strangers across Europe.

## New human-centered findings

### The headcounts were physical events

Rebecca Spang reconstructs the counting from diplomatic records. The French king's representative in Geneva counted girls as often as twice a week and sometimes twice a day. On 1 July 1784, he checked twenty-seven in the morning and more than one hundred women and girls later that day. In October, twenty-nine girls, all under seven, were gathered in his formal rooms. The immediate purpose was plain: establish that the named lives were still alive, roughly six months after the previous certification.

This is now the cold open because it begins with an action performed on the children, not a lecture about the French state.

### The famous thirty were a format, not one permanent group

Cramer documents pools of thirty, fifty, and sixty heads, with thirty the most common. Spang's records show more than one hundred named lives circulating through a city of roughly twenty thousand. The January 1782 sheet remains the episode's anchor, but the narration explicitly distinguishes it from the October 1784 group.

### The families made the girls legible

The system favored girls from affluent, interconnected households. Known parents, reliable birth records, family reputation, access to care, and the ability to locate a child later all mattered. The companion 1782 sheet names fathers because family identity was part of the infrastructure.

Spang identifies daughters and nieces from the families of Louis Bertrand, Horace-Bénédict de Saussure, Henri Paul Mallet, Gedeon Le Cointe, Gabriel Cramer, Samuel de Tournes, and others. The business expanded through kinship: daughters, sisters, nieces, granddaughters, and cousins.

### The girls did not control or sign the investment

Official tallies stated that the named women and girls had “no interest” in the annuities on their heads, so their signatures were not required. Spang gives a particularly clear episode from Strasbourg: an official certified three girls who had moved from Geneva, then explained that they had not signed because they could not read or write.

This detail should carry more weight than a generalized claim about exploitation. It shows the age and power imbalance without inventing the girls' feelings.

### Care and surveillance could overlap

Cramer's archival reconstruction says selected children could be medically monitored and inoculated against smallpox at shareholders' expense. Girls were preferred; exceptional boys could receive small pensions in exchange for remaining in Geneva and avoiding dangerous foreign service.

The script should not say every girl was inoculated or medically managed. “In some cases” is necessary.

### Growing up created paperwork

Proof-of-life certificates had to follow the named lives. Cramer records a 1793 annuity linked to Eléonore-Madeleine Bertrand, by then married to William Wickham, falling four semesters behind because certificates appear to have been lost on their way from England. Payments linked to Jacqueline Rolaz fell six semesters behind because her marriage had not been registered with the banker during a tax dispute involving her dowry.

These are from the wider Genevan cohorts, not necessarily the 1782 list. They show how an ordinary marriage or move became a banking problem.

### One face survives from the wider circle

The Metropolitan Museum of Art holds a public-domain plaster bust traditionally identified as Anne Audéoud of Geneva (1776–1840), first modeled around 1779–80. Spang identifies her as a banker's daughter within the broader circle of Genevan measuring lives and notes that she later married Gabriel Cramer.

Anne Audéoud is not Jeanne-Marguerite Audéoud from the January 1782 list. The script and edit state this directly. The bust is valuable because it lets viewers look at a child from the same system without fabricating a portrait.

### Pernette-Elisabeth Martin gives the story its early loss

The January 1782 list records Pernette-Elisabeth Martin, born 7 June 1780. She was about nineteen months old when listed. Cramer's archival reconstruction says she died at eight and that 212,197 livres, 3 sous, 2 deniers in annual rents attached to her life were extinguished.

The amount must remain attributed to Cramer and described as a historical reconstruction. The human fact comes first: a child died. The ledger consequence follows.

### Elisabeth-Pauline de Chapeaurouge gives it duration

The same 1782 list records Elisabeth-Pauline de Chapeaurouge, born 20 April 1778. A City of Geneva library research page identifies her lifespan as 1778–1862. She was three when listed and lived for nearly eighty more years.

There is not enough reliable biographical material to narrate her inner life, marriage, or daily experience. That silence is usable if handled plainly: the archive says far more about why her continued life mattered to investors than about what mattered to her.

- A long account of French war finance.
- A “hidden common risk” reveal.

The annuity rule needs roughly ninety seconds. The remaining time belongs to selection, care, counting, proof, movement, death, and longevity.

## Evidence standard for the human story

## What the instrument was

### Rente viagère

A rente viagère was a life annuity sold by the state:

1. An investor paid capital to the French government.
2. France promised an annual payment.
3. The payment continued only while a named person—the “head” or measuring life—remained alive.
4. When that person died, the payment ended.
5. The principal was generally not redeemed like a normal bond.

The investor and the named life did not have to be the same person. That separation created the opportunity.

### Why age mattered

If France paid the same annual rate to a contract tied to a 60-year-old and one tied to a healthy six-year-old, the younger life was likely to keep the payments alive much longer. Even when some issues used age bands, the schedules were coarse enough to create selection opportunities.

This is the core pricing error: the state sold duration cheaply to investors who could identify unusually durable measuring lives.

### Why girls

The research supports several overlapping reasons:

- Women generally had greater adult longevity than men.
- Known local families made identity and survival easier to verify.
- Children who had survived the most dangerous early years offered long potential payment periods.
- Surviving smallpox—or later being inoculated—removed one major mortality risk.
- Girls were less likely than boys to enter foreign military service or other hard-to-track occupations.

Do not reduce this to “girls live longer.” The commercial advantage was a combination of expected longevity, social visibility, medical knowledge, and administrative traceability.

## The thirty-head structure

Cramer describes a representative structure with 30,000 livres of annual rent split into parts of 30 livres. Each 30-livre part effectively contained one livre of annual income tied to each of thirty named lives.

- All thirty alive: 30 livres annual income per part.
- One death: 29 livres.
- Three deaths: 27 livres.
- Fifteen deaths: 15 livres.
- Last death: payment ends.

Each death reduced income rather than increasing the survivors’ shares. That is why the structure should not casually be called a tontine.

### Why pooling helped

A single-life annuity could be ruined by one child’s early death. A thirty-life basket made the outcome smoother. Investors no longer needed every child to survive. They needed the group’s survival pattern to be favorable on average.

The visual analogy is a portfolio of thirty light bulbs. One can go dark without turning off the room. But all thirty bulbs are still connected to the same power grid. In this story, France is the grid.

## Illustrative economics

This table is a teaching device, not a reconstruction of an actual investor’s realized return.

Assume France promises annual payments equal to 10 percent of principal, the payment is level, the discount rate is 5 percent, and there is no default, commission, exchange cost, tax, or mortality uncertainty.

| Payment duration | Present value of promised payments per 1 livre invested |
|---:|---:|
| 5 years | 0.433 |
| 10 years | 0.772 |
| 20 years | 1.246 |
| 40 years | 1.716 |
| 60 years | 1.893 |

The same nominal rate becomes much more valuable when the measuring life can keep it running for decades. A sophisticated investor did not need secret mathematics to see this. The difficult work was finding, monitoring, and legally documenting the right lives at scale.

### What this calculation omits

- Probability of death in each year
- French default or restructuring
- Delayed remittance
- Currency conversion and depreciation
- Bank commissions
- Legal-title disputes
- Political closure of payment channels
- The investor’s financing cost
- The value of any amortization withholding

Never present the simple table as a promised profit.

## How the market was assembled

### 1. France needed cash

Eighteenth-century war and a weak fiscal system made the monarchy a persistent borrower. Life annuities offered a politically and commercially attractive way to raise capital without promising a conventional principal repayment date.

### 2. Investors could nominate someone else

The payment could be measured by a third party’s life. That transformed an individual retirement-like contract into a selection problem: whose life would keep the stream alive longest?

### 3. Genevan bankers had an information advantage

Geneva was small, commercially connected, and socially legible. Bankers could identify families, know birth dates and health histories, and obtain continuing proof that a child remained alive.

### 4. Banks pooled the lives

The thirty-name lists spread idiosyncratic death risk. The structure became divisible enough for many beneficial owners to participate, including people far below the very wealthiest tier.

### 5. Banks supplied the plumbing

They subscribed in Paris, sold participations, collected remittances, charged commissions, tracked deaths, and gathered certificates of life. Some arrangements used deferred bills or the annuity income itself to help finance the purchase.

### 6. The shared risk remained

Every slice still depended on the French government’s willingness and ability to honor or fairly convert the payments. Diversification across girls was not diversification across sovereigns.

## Scale: what can be said safely

Cramer estimated approximately 10.3 million livres of annual French life-rent placed in Geneva, corresponding to approximately 113.8 million livres of capital. He called his estimate incomplete but believed it close to the real order of magnitude.

He also reproduced a January 1793 reckoning of roughly 100.6 million livres in total annual French life-rent outstanding across one-, two-, three-, and four-head forms, associated with roughly 1.09 billion livres of capital.

These are historical estimates and classifications, not modern audited totals. They are useful to establish that life annuities were material. They do not prove that the thirty-maiden pools represented most French debt or most Genevan wealth.

## Human reality

The primary 1782 sheet names thirty individual girls and gives their birth dates. A companion sheet also identifies parents. This matters for the story: the underlying “data” were real children whose survival had to be certified.

The archive complicates a popular simplification. The January 1782 list was not limited to girls aged four to seven; its birth dates indicate a wider range. The correct language is “young Genevan girls” unless the script is discussing a particular documented cohort.

Cramer describes:

- A preference for prominent or well-known families.
- Monitoring by doctors and bankers.
- Inoculation against smallpox in some arrangements.
- Certificates of life for nominees who moved away.
- Early deaths that immediately cut the income attached to a cohort.
- Occasional use of boys, sometimes paired with incentives to remain in Geneva or avoid dangerous service.

The girls did not design or purchase the product. Their families, bankers, investors, and the French state did. Keep the narration focused on institutional incentives, not on turning the children into a macabre spectacle.

## The Necker question

Necker is central but easy to misuse.

### Safe

- He was a Genevan banker who became the monarchy’s leading finance official.
- His administrations relied heavily on borrowing.
- Life annuities were part of the funding environment he managed.
- His Genevan background is narratively important because the investor network was also Genevan.

### Unsafe without new evidence

- “Necker invented the Thirty Maidens.”
- “Necker personally selected the girls.”
- “Necker designed the exact 1782 pool.”
- “Necker’s scheme caused the French Revolution.”

Cramer explicitly warns that later writers may have misread the evidence when attributing a multi-head invention to Necker.

## What went wrong

The product contained at least four distinct layers of risk.

| Risk | Was it diversified by thirty lives? | Result |
|---|---|---|
| One child dies unexpectedly | Yes, substantially | Income falls by only one thirtieth of that basket |
| The whole cohort has worse mortality than expected | Partly | Pool smooths randomness but cannot erase systematic mortality |
| Bank or agent fails, records are disputed, remittance breaks | No | Beneficial owners can lose access even if France nominally owes the annuity |
| France restructures, pays in depreciated money, or changes the legal regime | No | Every life in the basket shares the same sovereign exposure |

The most revealing failure was not actuarial. It was categorical. The investors optimized the visible micro-risk and remained exposed to the macro-risk.

## Did the scheme help cause the French Revolution?

**Answer: not in the simple viral-video sense.**

The monarchy’s debt burden and inability to reform taxation were central to the fiscal crisis that led to the Estates-General. Life annuities were expensive and could worsen borrowing costs when badly priced. Genevan selection made that weakness more costly to the state.

But:

- France had many debt instruments and much larger structural fiscal problems.
- War spending, tax privileges, political vetoes, administrative fragmentation, and credibility all mattered.
- The Thirty Maidens were a mechanism inside the crisis, not a sufficient cause of it.
- Saying “thirty girls bankrupted France” converts a good story into a false monocausal claim.

A defensible line is: “The thirty names did not cause the Revolution. They reveal why the monarchy’s debt machine could raise cash today by making promises it did not fully understand.”

## Was it a tontine?

Usually, no.

In a classic tontine, the income allocated to dead participants is redistributed among survivors, so the surviving shares grow. In the thirty-head structure described by Cramer, a death reduced the total annual payment attached to the basket. The remaining lives did not absorb that dead life’s payment.

Use:

- pooled life-annuity interests
- thirty-head annuity basket
- fund-like participation
- longevity-linked government debt

Avoid using “tontine” as the main label.

## Was it the first securitization?

Treat this as an analogy, not a verdict.

The arrangement had securitization-like features:

- a pool of cash-flow-generating contracts
- divisible interests sold to multiple investors
- an arranger and administrator
- risk transformed through pooling
- information and servicing infrastructure

It lacked many features of modern securitization and did not necessarily use a bankruptcy-remote special-purpose vehicle. Recent scholarship is more comfortable calling it an early investment fund. The title “the first securitization” would create an avoidable taxonomy fight.

## Revised narrative structure

### 0:00–1:10 — The count

Open inside the diplomat's reception rooms in October 1784. Twenty-nine girls under seven are inspected for one fact: whether they are alive.

### 1:10–2:20 — The list

Reveal the January 1782 document. Read several names, then introduce Pernette-Elisabeth Martin and Elisabeth-Pauline de Chapeaurouge. Clarify that the famous thirty were repeated cohorts, not one fixed lineup.

### 2:20–3:40 — The rule

Explain the life annuity, the separation between investor and named life, and the simple reason for pooling siblings or thirty children. Stop the finance explanation here.

### 3:40–5:20 — Selection

Known families, access to care, preference for girls, smallpox inoculation in some arrangements, and rare boys paid to avoid danger. End with the Strasbourg record: girls carrying financial claims who could not sign their names.

### 5:20–6:40 — A face

Use the Met bust of Anne Audéoud with the explicit cohort clarification. Let the portrait interrupt the tidy language of “heads.”

### 6:40–8:05 — Growing up

Proof-of-life certificates follow women who marry or move. Use Eléonore Bertrand's lost English certificate and Jacqueline Rolaz's unregistered marriage.

### 8:05–9:35 — Pernette

Return to the exact 1782 line. Pernette dies at eight. The family loss and ledger consequence occupy the same event without being treated as equivalent.

### 9:35–11:00 — Elisabeth-Pauline

Another line on the same sheet remains alive until 1862. Do not fabricate the missing biography; let the documentary imbalance become visible.

### 11:00–12:10 — The list again

Return to all thirty names. End with the girls changing while the paper keeps them at their childhood ages.

## Narrative guardrails

- The October 1784 gathering is not presented as the exact January 1782 cohort.
- Anne Audéoud is from the broader system, not the January 1782 list.
- The girls are never described as investors, willing participants, property, or collateral.
- Medical care and inoculation occurred in some arrangements, not necessarily for every child.
- No invented reaction, dialogue, domestic scene, or private feeling is assigned to a named girl.
- Pernette's death is a family event before it is a financial number.
- Elisabeth-Pauline's missing biography is acknowledged, not filled with generic womanhood.
- The conclusion ends on the girls. It does not pivot to a modern portfolio lesson.

## Do-not-overclaim guardrails

Do not say:

- The thirty girls were collateral.
- Investors owned the girls.
- Investors profited when a girl died.
- Necker invented the scheme.
- Every girl was inoculated.
- Every cohort had exactly the same contract.
- All thirty were four to seven years old.
- The pool was a tontine.
- It was definitively the first securitization.
- It bankrupted France.
- It caused the French Revolution.
- The scheme captured 90 percent of Geneva’s wealth.
- The investors were guaranteed 10 percent returns.
- The girls’ survival protected the investment from default or inflation.
