# Thirty Maidens of Geneva — source ledger

**Project:** France Bet Its Debt on 30 Little Girls  
**Date compiled:** 2026-08-30  
**Freshness cutoff:** 2026-08-30  
**Companion dossier:** [2026-08-30-thirty-maidens-of-geneva.md](./2026-08-30-thirty-maidens-of-geneva.md)

This ledger separates evidence for historical claims from material that is merely useful for illustration. Page numbers below refer to the printed pagination where available. All translations from French should be checked against the original before recording narration.

## Source hierarchy

- **Tier A — primary:** contemporary lists, contracts, edicts, accounts, and archival reproductions.
- **Tier B — scholarly:** peer-reviewed or specialist historical research with citations.
- **Tier C — orientation:** museum, heritage, magazine, and popular explanations. Useful for discovery, not for carrying a disputed claim alone.
- **Asset only:** useful visual or video material that is not evidence for the historical argument.

## Tier A — primary documents and archival reproductions

### A1. 1782 list of the thirty Genevan girls

- **Title:** Désignation des trente demoiselles genevoises sur les têtes desquelles les rentes viagères de la création de janvier 1782 ont été constituées
- **Repository:** Bibliothèque de Genève / e-rara
- **Catalogue record and viewer:** https://www.e-rara.ch/gep_r/content/zoom/31278574
- **Full-resolution repository image:** https://www.e-rara.ch/gep_r/download/webcache/0/31278574
- **OCR:** https://www.e-rara.ch/gep_r/content/pagetext/31278574
- **Shelf mark:** BGE Gf 1341/3/2303
- **Supports:** A January 1782 issue really was constituted on thirty specifically named Genevan girls; the list gives their birth dates.
- **High-value visual:** The strongest cold-open document. Begin on the heading, then reveal the names and dates.
- **Important nuance:** The dates imply a wider age range than the often-repeated “four to seven” shorthand. For this list, the youngest was under one and the oldest was over eight in January 1782. Say “young girls” unless discussing another documented cohort.
- **Rights:** The underlying 1782 document is public domain by age. Confirm e-rara’s current reproduction and attribution terms before exporting the scan; credit Bibliothèque de Genève and the shelf mark in any case.

- **Title:** Désignation des trente têtes choisies pour les rentes viagères de la création du mois de janvier 1782
- **Repository:** Bibliothèque de Genève / e-rara
- **Catalogue record and viewer:** https://www.e-rara.ch/gep_r/content/zoom/31278744
- **Full-resolution repository image:** https://www.e-rara.ch/gep_r/download/webcache/0/31278744
- **OCR:** https://www.e-rara.ch/gep_r/content/pagetext/31278744

- **Rights:** Same caveat and credit as A1.

### A3. Jacques Necker, Compte rendu au Roi, 1781

- **Internet Archive copy:** https://archive.org/details/compterenduauroi00neck_0
- **Alternate scan:** https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_compte-rendu-au-roi-par_necker-jacques_1781
- **Supports:** Contemporary official presentation of royal finances and Necker’s public-finance programme.
- **Does not prove:** That Necker invented the thirty-head scheme, that the report honestly captured all war borrowing, or that the scheme caused the Revolution.
- **Visual use:** Cover, engraved tables, and typography for the section on the monarchy selling credibility.
- **Rights:** 1781 text is public domain. Verify the host’s scan-use statement and credit the holding library.

### A4. State Archives of Geneva notarial records

- **Finding route cited by Cramer:** Archives d’État de Genève, including Commerce J 1–8 and the Minutaire de rentes viagères.
- **Entry point:** https://archives-etat-ge.ch/
- **Supports:** Contracts, bank syndicates, beneficial owners, bills, commissions, proof-of-life administration, and later liquidation.
- **Status:** Not inspected directly for this dossier. Cramer’s 1946 article transcribes and aggregates these records.
- **Next action for a flagship production:** Ask the archive for digitized examples of a participation contract, certificate of life, and a bank’s subscriber list. These would materially improve the film.
- **Rights:** Determine item-level reproduction terms with the archive.

## Tier B — scholarship

### B1. Marc Cramer, “Les trente Demoiselles de Genève et les billets solidaires” (1946)

- **Journal:** Swiss Journal of Economics and Statistics, vol. 82, issue 2, pp. 109–138
- **PDF:** https://www.sjes.ch/papers/1946-II-2.pdf
- **Index:** https://ideas.repec.org/a/ses/arsjes/1946-ii-2.html
- **Why it matters:** The richest single reconstruction, based heavily on Genevan archival and notarial material.
- **Claims it can carry:**
  - France sold life annuities whose payments ended when the named life died.
  - Rates were often flat across broad ages and could be around 9–10 percent for some issues.
  - Genevan banks organized repeated speculative waves and sold divisible participations.
  - A 30,000-livre annual rent could be split into 30-livre parts; one death reduced each part’s income by one thirtieth.
  - Shareholders and banks monitored nominees, obtained certificates of life, and sometimes financed inoculation.
  - Cramer estimated roughly 10.3 million livres of annual French life-rent placed in Geneva, corresponding to roughly 113.8 million livres of capital, while acknowledging incompleteness.
  - The revolutionary settlement, currency depreciation, legal-title confusion, and bank failures impaired the investments.
- **Pages to capture:** 109–117 for mechanism and cohorts; 118–121 for administration and financing; 126–132 for revolutionary liquidation and bank failures.
- **Limitations:** Old scholarship; strong authorial judgments; some totals are estimates; the exact cohorts and contract structures varied. Do not turn every example into a universal feature.
- **Critical correction:** Cramer explicitly finds no solid basis for saying Necker invented a seven-head or thirty-head scheme. Use Necker as a major issuer and Genevan insider, not as an inventor unless new primary evidence is found.

### B2. François R. Velde and David R. Weir, “The Financial Market and Government Debt Policy in France, 1746–1793” (1992)

- **Journal page:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-economic-history/article/abs/financial-market-and-government-debt-policy-in-france-17461793/15A623836DA5D950BA79E33E1781FA7A
- **Author-hosted PDF:** https://people.bu.edu/chamley/HSFref/VeldeWeirJEH92.pdf
- **Why it matters:** Leading modern economic-history treatment of French debt policy and pricing.
- **Claims it can carry:** Flat or coarse annuity pricing made some ages especially attractive; naming third parties allowed investors to select young lives; Genevan banks reduced proof-of-life and idiosyncratic mortality frictions by maintaining lists and Paris connections.
- **Use in argument:** Establishes that the product was not simply a bizarre bet. It was a response to pricing and transaction-cost problems in the French debt market.
- **Limitations:** The article studies the wider debt market; not every conclusion is specific to the thirty girls.

### B3. François R. Velde, “The Case of the Undying Debt” (2009)

- **PDF:** https://piketty-backend.pse.ens.fr/files/capitalisback/CountryData/France/Other/PublicDebtFR/Velde09.pdf
- **Why it matters:** Direct treatment of how revolutionary France converted Old Regime life annuities and the long-lived claims that persisted.
- **Claims to verify before script:** Cambon’s March 1794 conversion method, the treatment of life-contingent claims, and the survival of obligations long after regime change.
- **Status:** Located, but the hosted PDF was intermittently unavailable during this research pass. Treat exact numbers or dates from it as unresolved until the file is downloaded and checked.

### B4. “More on the Origin of Financial Economics: Early Contributions to Joint Life Annuity Valuation” (2024)

- **Journal page:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-history-of-economic-thought/article/more-on-the-origin-of-financial-economics-early-contributions-to-joint-life-annuity-valuation/5978E32EDF91D3C7872A494BA8C34207
- **Why it matters:** Recent scholarship placing the Genevan arrangements in the history of pooled investment and joint-life valuation.
- **Claims it can carry:** Genevan banks sold fund-like shares from the early 1770s into the late 1780s; nominees were young and often chosen from known families; the arrangements belong in the prehistory of financial economics.
- **Caveat:** “Early investment fund” is supported. “First securitization” is a modern analogy and should be framed as a question, not a settled classification.

### B5. Rebecca L. Spang, Stuff and Money in the Time of the French Revolution (2015)

- **Publisher:** Harvard University Press
- **Preview route:** https://www.perlego.com/book/1133245/stuff-and-money-in-the-time-of-the-french-revolution-pdf
- **Why it matters:** Connects the contracts to revolutionary money, material culture, and the named lives behind ostensibly abstract claims.
- **Useful details to verify in the print edition:** Families used daughters, nieces, and cousins; women were preferred partly for longevity; state and bankers maintained proof-of-life systems.
- **Limitations:** Obtain page-accurate citations from a library copy before narration.

### B6. Pierre de Saint-Phalle, Jacques Necker and the Fiscal Origins of the French Revolution

- **Project page:** https://pierredesaintphalle.com/book.html
- **Why it matters:** Orientation to Necker and late-monarchy fiscal structure.
- **Limitations:** Use the book or underlying archival citations—not the promotional page—for exact budget ratios.

### B7. Chicago Fed, “Life annuities and the French public debt” context (2024)

- **Article:** https://www.chicagofed.org/publications/economic-perspectives/2024/2
- **Why it matters:** Clear modern explanation of life annuities, no-principal-repayment structure, and their place in eighteenth-century French finance.
- **Use:** Mechanism check and broader context.
- **Limitations:** Not a source for the exact Genevan cohort or bank contracts.

## Sources not safe to carry alone

- Popular claims that the thirty girls represented “90 percent of Geneva’s wealth.”
- Claims that Necker invented the scheme.
- Claims that the product bankrupted France or caused the Revolution.
- Claims that every cohort contained girls aged four to seven.
- Claims that this was literally a tontine.
- Claims that all contracts were one standardized security.
- Claims that inoculation guaranteed survival.
- Any precise return calculation that ignores commissions, remittance, exchange, payment suspension, depreciation, or revolutionary conversion.
