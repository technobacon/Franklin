# Franklin Voice-Diagnostic Rubric

Use this after a complete draft and before the single targeted voice revision. It is a directional editorial instrument, not an authorship detector and not a scientific probability that a passage was AI-generated.

## Method

1. Read the relevant authenticated voice samples and accepted Franklin passages.
2. Read the script aloud or simulate a deliberate spoken read.
3. Score each dimension from 1 to 5.
4. Quote the five to ten lines with the largest, most consequential deviations.
5. Explain the deviation before proposing any fix.
6. Revise only the identified lines and any joins the changes disturb.
7. Re-score once. Do not enter an indefinite “make it more human” loop.

Score meaning:

- **5 — aligned:** strongly resembles the available Franklin voice evidence.
- **4 — sound:** minor drift that does not interrupt the voice.
- **3 — mixed:** noticeable model-default habits or uncertain corpus match.
- **2 — weak:** repeated drift that affects a chapter or argument.
- **1 — incompatible:** the passage contradicts established voice evidence or sounds implausible aloud.

Treat an average below 4, or any score below 3, as a reason for targeted revision. Do not chase a perfect total by flattening unusual but authentic language.

## The ten dimensions

### 1. Sentence rhythm

Ask whether length, syntax, and pressure vary like human explanation rather than staying uniformly polished.

High score:

- some thoughts arrive quickly;
- some take a sentence or two to develop;
- pauses and fragments occur where the idea demands them;
- complexity changes with the material.

Low score:

- sentences repeatedly land at similar lengths;
- every paragraph has the same cadence;
- a mechanical short-long-short pattern has been imposed;
- every sentence feels equally finished.

### 2. Structural predictability

Ask whether the listener can hear the model's template before the next line arrives.

High score:

- chapters have distinct jobs;
- section shapes vary with the argument;
- the prose can cut on a strong idea;
- examples and qualifications appear when needed.

Low score:

- setup, three examples, mini-conclusion repeats;
- every turn is announced;
- each section previews and then summarizes itself;
- conclusions resolve more neatly than the evidence.

### 3. Concrete versus abstract language

Ask whether actors do things, or whether concepts undergo processes.

High score:

- “banks stopped getting coins back”;
- “customers kept quarters at home”;
- mechanisms use named actors and verbs.

Low score:

- noun-heavy sentences;
- “the emergence of,” “a reduction in,” or “the transformation of”;
- vague terms such as dynamics, landscape, shift, framework, or implications standing in for an effect.

### 4. Personal stance

Ask whether supplied judgments remain visible without fabricated autobiography.

High score:

- the script preserves the creator's skepticism, surprise, objections, humour, and priorities;
- opinions are traceable to supplied notes;
- uncertainty is specific.

Low score:

- every fact receives the same polite interest;
- the script invents feelings or experiences;
- generic “this matters” commentary replaces an actual judgment;
- the narrator seems to have no pattern of attention.

### 5. Signposting

Ask whether transitions do real work.

High score:

- a bridge resolves genuine ambiguity, time movement, or a causal jump;
- adjacent thoughts often connect without announcement.

Low score:

- “to understand this, we first need to”;
- “but there is another part of the story”;
- “this brings us to the most important point”;
- “so what does all this mean?”;
- topic sentences narrate the document architecture rather than the subject.

### 6. Rhetorical-template frequency

Ask whether a device has become a house rhythm.

High score:

- rhetorical questions, contrast, triplets, em dashes, and fragments appear only when useful;
- several paragraphs can pass without a polished turn of phrase.

Low score:

- repeated “not X, but Y” constructions;
- repeated one-word questions with answers;
- frequent groups of three;
- repeated quote-shaped aphorisms;
- jokes and reveals use the same syntax.

Do not penalize one isolated construction. Look for reliance.

### 7. Vocabulary authenticity

Ask whether the diction belongs to the creator's samples and to spoken Franklin narration.

High score:

- plain terms remain plain;
- necessary finance vocabulary is introduced through mechanism;
- ordinary repetition is preserved.

Low score:

- synonym rotation for its own sake;
- ornamental words the creator does not use;
- academic or corporate abstractions;
- slang, contractions, or profanity inserted merely to perform humanness.

### 8. Natural repetition

Ask whether repeated words clarify the mechanism or whether duplicated ideas pad the runtime.

High score:

- the same precise noun or verb can recur;
- a key phrase returns only when its meaning changes;
- the listener is trusted to remember recent explanation.

Low score:

- the thesaurus hides useful lexical repetition;
- the same conclusion reappears in new wording;
- the next chapter repeats the prior chapter's ending;
- the thesis is restated after every example.

### 9. Spoken pronounceability

Ask whether a person could say the line cleanly on the intended read.

High score:

- clauses arrive in an order the speaker can hold;
- qualifications sit beside their claims;
- numbers are framed by the question they answer;
- breaths and emphasis feel natural.

Low score:

- stacked subordinate clauses;
- long noun phrases;
- page-smart but mouth-hostile wording;
- unexplained acronym or number clusters;
- lines the creator will instinctively paraphrase.

### 10. Conclusion neatness

Ask whether the ending earns its confidence.

High score:

- it changes the viewer's mental model;
- it lands on a consequence, trade-off, or unresolved tension;
- the final line grows directly from the evidence.

Low score:

- section-by-section recap;
- optimistic uplift;
- “broader lesson” language without a specific lesson;
- new significance introduced at the last moment;
- a slogan that is cleaner than the argument.

## Required diagnostic output

### Scorecard

Report all ten scores and a one-sentence basis for each. Give the average to one decimal place, while stating that it is editorial judgment rather than an objective measurement.

### Worst-line report

For each of five to ten lines:

- quote the exact line;
- name the main dimension affected;
- explain why it differs from the voice evidence;
- identify the smallest useful change;
- do not rewrite it unless revision was requested.

### Pattern summary

Name no more than three script-wide patterns. Separate a repeated problem from an isolated stylistic choice.

### Revision boundary

State which passages may change and which strong passages should remain untouched. This prevents the revision from becoming a global polish pass.

## Read-aloud evidence

During the creator's read:

- highlight any sentence they instinctively paraphrase;
- record the spontaneous replacement verbatim;
- treat the replacement as stronger voice evidence than a generic preference adjective;
- add accepted examples to `reference/voice-corpus.md` with source and date.

A line may be factually precise and stylistically wrong. Fixing voice must not weaken the claim, remove a necessary qualification, or introduce unsupported causality.
