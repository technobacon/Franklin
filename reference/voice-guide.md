# Franklin — Voice Guide

Read `reference/scriptwriting-direction.md` for the full writing doctrine and `reference/voice-corpus.md` for actual voice evidence. When they differ, authenticated voice samples outrank general style labels.

## Target voice

Knowledgeable, conversational, precise, and dry. Franklin sounds like somebody who enjoys the mechanism enough to explain it clearly and distrusts any explanation that becomes suspiciously tidy.

The narration should feel like one person with a pattern of attention. It may be amused, skeptical, surprised, annoyed, suspicious, or occasionally blunt. It should never sound like a textbook summary wearing jokes as a hat.

Do not interpret “human” as casual, slang-heavy, grammatically damaged, or constantly informal. The target is how the creator naturally explains an idea when he knows what he is talking about.

## Voice priority

Use this order:

1. the creator's current instruction;
2. authenticated natural-speech and read-aloud samples;
3. chapter-specific creator notes and judgments;
4. accepted Franklin passages;
5. this guide;
6. adjectives such as analytical, conversational, dry, or witty.

Examples outrank adjectives. Preserve supplied opinions and emotional reactions. Never invent a personal experience, feeling, or conviction.

## Rhythm

Human speech is not statistically tidy.

- Allow substantial variation in sentence length and construction.
- Use a short sentence when the thought actually lands there.
- Allow another one.
- Let some explanations develop through a longer sentence or an aside.
- Use fragments when they are natural to the voice evidence.
- Do not normalize paragraphs toward equal size or polish.
- Do not manufacture a predictable short-long-short pattern.
- Reserve dramatic fragments for genuine turns; too many become another template.

AI prose often sounds acceptable sentence by sentence and artificial over thirty sentences. Judge the distribution across a chapter.

## Language

- Write for the ear.
- Prefer concrete actors and active verbs: “banks held,” “depositors withdrew,” “customers kept,” “the central bank bought.”
- Use abstract nouns only when the concept requires them.
- Introduce terminology after the viewer understands the practical problem it names.
- Put the important qualification beside the claim, not three paragraphs later.
- Use the actual interesting fact before commentary about why it is interesting.
- Introduce a number through the question it answers.
- Allow ordinary words to repeat. Do not rotate synonyms merely to avoid repetition.
- Do not enlarge a simple mechanism into a theme, shift, landscape, dynamic, framework, or transformation.
- Allow a strong line to end without explaining its emotional significance.

Prefer:

> Banks weren't getting coins back.

Avoid:

> The return flow of coinage into banking institutions declined.

## Structure

Each chapter needs a distinct job. It does not need a miniature introduction, body, and conclusion.

- Do not announce every transition.
- Do not preview a point the viewer is about to hear.
- Do not summarize a chapter merely because it ended.
- Do not open the next chapter by repeating the prior conclusion.
- Do not automatically give every claim three examples.
- Let one thought lead into the next when the logic is already clear.
- Use a transition when chronology, actor, or causal direction would otherwise be confusing.
- End on the strongest line when the point is complete.

Example:

> The coins hadn't vanished. They'd stopped circulating.

Cut.

## Common model-default patterns

Rewrite a passage that repeatedly relies on:

- “It is not X; it is Y” or close variants masquerading as insight.
- “It is not just X, but Y.”
- “Here is the thing.”
- “But here is where it gets interesting.”
- “So what does this mean?”
- A one-word rhetorical question followed by its answer: “The result? Panic.”
- Repeated groups of three.
- Repeated section conclusions that restate the thesis.
- “Let that sink in,” “at its core,” “in today's world,” or “it is important to note.”
- Vague importance language such as “plays a crucial role” when the actual effect can be named.
- Empty transitions such as “moreover,” “furthermore,” “additionally,” or “with that said.”
- Inflated words such as “pivotal,” “groundbreaking,” “transformative,” “multifaceted,” “robust,” “seamless,” “delve,” “unpack,” “landscape,” or “tapestry.”
- A conclusion that announces a “broader lesson” without identifying one.

No device or word is universally banned. One natural em dash or triplet is not evidence of a problem. Repetitive reliance is the problem.

Do not repair these patterns by swapping synonyms. Rebuild the sentence around a concrete claim.

## Originality and intellectual ownership

Generic correctness is not enough.

Generic:

> This demonstrates how disruptions in circulation can create shortages even when aggregate supply remains sufficient.

Franklin:

> America didn't really run out of coins. In a bizarre way, it ran out of moving coins.

The second line notices something. That is where voice lives.

Before drafting, preserve the creator's unpolished judgments: which detail feels strange, which popular explanation is incomplete, what should not be overstated, which comparison matters, and where a joke may exist. Develop that attention. Do not fabricate it.

## Dry humour

Humour should usually emerge from:

1. **Institutional understatement** — a formal term describing something plainly chaotic.
2. **Incentive collision** — each actor behaves rationally and the group produces nonsense.
3. **Scale mismatch** — a tiny assumption moves an absurd amount of money.
4. **Franklin's predicament** — Franklin watches later financial inventions use his face as moral support.

Keep punchlines short. Do not explain them, stack several jokes on one beat, or interrupt a section involving real harm to prove the video is funny.

Good:

> The contract called this a “liquidity event,” which is a dignified way of saying everybody wanted the same exit.

Weak:

> Buckle up, because this wild financial rollercoaster is about to get crazy.

## Gaming references

Gaming references are seasoning, not a quota.

- Use one when a mechanic provides a clean structural analogy: faucets and sinks, auction-house liquidity, item duplication, min-maxing, principal-agent problems, or coordination failures.
- Explain the mapping in one sentence so a non-player follows it.
- Prefer widely legible mechanics over proper nouns.
- Zero references is valid.
- Do not use gaming slang as the explanation.
- Return to the financial mechanism quickly.

## Technical explanations

A useful default order is:

1. practical problem;
2. actors and incentives;
3. mechanism;
4. formal term;
5. numerical or historical test;
6. limitation or counterexample.

This is not a paragraph template. Change the order when curiosity or chronology demands it. Avoid analogy chains: one accurate model tested against evidence is stronger than five clever comparisons.

## Delivery notation

Use sparingly:

- `[beat]` for one deliberate pause.
- `[hold]` for a rare longer silence.
- `[quick] ... [/quick]` for short acceleration.
- `[slow] ... [/slow]` for a weighted reveal.
- *Italics* for the single word carrying vocal emphasis.
- `»` before the handful of lines the performance and edit must land.

Unmarked narration should already sound natural.

## Revision rule

Do not run repeated requests to make a draft “more human.”

Use:

1. factual and structural draft;
2. specific voice diagnosis;
3. one targeted revision;
4. creator read-aloud.

Quote the worst lines and name the deviation before changing them. Preserve strong passages and authentic irregularities.

## Final voice test

Before delivery, ask:

- Does each chapter add a new thought?
- Could this line belong to any finance video?
- Did the narration preserve the creator's supplied pattern of attention?
- Did a qualification become a vague hedge?
- Are actors doing things, or are abstract concepts undergoing processes?
- Did the script circle a point because the next causal step was never researched?
- Are the sentence lengths and paragraph shapes suspiciously uniform?
- Did a rhetorical device become a house rhythm?
- Is ordinary repetition being hidden by a thesaurus?
- Would the creator instinctively paraphrase this line aloud?
- Does the ending earn its confidence?
- Would a smart viewer feel spoken with rather than spoken down to?
