# Franklin — Voice Corpus

## Purpose

This file stores evidence of how the creator actually speaks and which generated choices he accepts or rejects. It is not a showcase of polished writing.

A passage belongs here when the creator says, “I would actually say this,” or when it is captured from natural speech or a spontaneous read-aloud substitution. A passage does not qualify merely because it sounds good.

## Current status

The corpus does not yet contain a full natural-speech transcript. Until it does, use the creator-approved decision boundaries below, the existing Franklin voice guide, and chapter-specific raw notes. Do not pretend that these materials provide a complete personal style model.

## Evidence classes

Use these labels when adding material:

- **NS — Natural speech:** an unperformed explanation, conversation, or voice note.
- **RS — Read-aloud substitution:** wording the creator says spontaneously instead of the drafted line.
- **AS — Accepted script:** a scripted passage the creator confirms he would naturally say.
- **DB — Decision boundary:** an accepted/rejected pair with an explanation.
- **CN — Creator note:** an unpolished judgment about what matters, what feels wrong, or how a chapter should land.

Natural speech and read-aloud substitutions are the strongest evidence.

## Seed decision boundaries

These examples were supplied as direction for Franklin. They are useful boundaries, but they are not substitutes for a natural-speech corpus.

### DB-001 — Mechanism over manufactured significance

Rejected:

> This was not merely a shortage of coins—it was a symptom of a deeper transformation in how Americans interacted with money.

Accepted:

> America had enough coins. The problem was that they were in the wrong places.

Why:

- The rejected line uses negative parallelism as a ready-made reveal.
- It turns a specific distribution failure into an abstract transformation.
- It tries to manufacture a quotation.
- The accepted version uses ordinary words and names the mechanism.

### DB-002 — Actors and verbs over nominalization

Rejected:

> The emergence of this phenomenon, reflecting broader shifts in consumer behavior, highlights the increasingly complex relationship between monetary circulation and evolving payment preferences.

Accepted:

> People hadn't stopped using money. They'd stopped moving coins around in the way the system expected them to.

Why:

- The rejected version contains no useful actor.
- Abstract nouns do the work that people, banks, and coins should do.
- “Broader shifts” and “complex relationship” add importance without adding mechanism.
- The accepted version explains the behavioral change directly.

### DB-003 — Interpretation over generic commentary

Rejected:

> This demonstrates how disruptions in circulation can create shortages even when aggregate supply remains sufficient.

Accepted:

> So America didn't really run out of coins. In a bizarre way, it ran out of moving coins.

Why:

- The rejected line is correct but could close almost any distribution-system explainer.
- The accepted line identifies the oddity and gives it a specific interpretation.
- The short second sentence is earned by the mechanism.

### DB-004 — Spoken thought over compressed formal prose

Rejected:

> This outcome primarily results from the interaction between supply constraints and changes in circulation velocity.

Accepted:

> And this is where it gets weird, because there wasn't actually a shortage of coins. There were plenty of coins. They were just… somewhere else.

Why:

- The rejected sentence compresses several causal questions into abstract vocabulary.
- The accepted passage follows the order in which a speaker would reveal the contradiction.
- The pause and plain repetition serve the thought. They are not decoration.

## Seed creator-note patterns

These are examples of useful raw input for future chapters:

- “This is genuinely insane to me.”
- “Don't overstate this—technically the Fed couldn't control X.”
- “I want the viewer to initially think there weren't enough coins.”
- “The interesting comparison here is inventories, not money supply.”
- “Maybe a joke about people collectively keeping $50 billion in their sofa cushions.”
- “I hate the usual explanation because it skips this part.”
- “This sounds important but is mostly irrelevant.”
- “The obvious interpretation is X. I think Y is more interesting.”

Treat the wording as raw evidence, not required catchphrases. Never insert a supplied note automatically. Develop the judgment only where the facts support it.

## Collection protocol

Record 20–30 minutes of natural speech across several topics. This is a practical collection target, not a scientific minimum.

Useful prompts include:

- explain a financial or economic story you find interesting;
- explain why a familiar explanation is incomplete;
- explain a game mechanic that produces a bad incentive;
- explain why a film, book, or video worked;
- explain a concept to one specific intelligent friend.

Do not perform a finished script. Concentrate on the idea.

Transcribe the recording with false starts and repetitions preserved initially. Remove only material that is unusable for privacy, factual contamination, or transcription failure.

## Admission test

Before adding a sample, confirm:

- the creator produced or explicitly approved it;
- the source type and date are recorded;
- the wording is exact or clearly marked as lightly cleaned;
- private employer or client information is absent;
- the passage represents spoken identity, not merely admired prose;
- enough surrounding context remains to understand the habit.

## Analysis dimensions

Once the corpus contains enough authenticated material, analyze it for:

- sentence-length distribution, including the long tail;
- fragments and incomplete thoughts;
- contractions;
- connective phrases;
- rhetorical questions;
- qualification frequency and placement;
- example introductions;
- joke construction;
- direct address;
- first-person usage;
- formality;
- parenthetical thoughts and asides;
- ordinary lexical repetition;
- chapter openings and endings;
- preferred verbs and nouns;
- vocabulary and constructions that are conspicuously absent.

Describe distributions and tendencies. Do not turn every observed habit into an absolute rule.

## Maintenance

Add new evidence after each creator read-aloud. Prefer a small number of high-confidence samples over a large mixed corpus.

When a new sample conflicts with an older one:

1. check whether the context or emotional register differs;
2. prefer natural speech over an accepted script;
3. prefer recent repeated behavior over one isolated example;
4. preserve both when they represent legitimate range;
5. update the voice specification only when the pattern is stable.

Never infer private biographical claims, professional experiences, or personal convictions from style samples.
